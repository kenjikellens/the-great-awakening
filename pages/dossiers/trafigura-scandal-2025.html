Trafigura Bribery Scandal (2025)

A dossier on the landmark January 2025 conviction of the global commodities trading giant Trafigura by the Swiss Federal Criminal Court for bribing Angolan officials.

The Bribery Scheme

In January 2025, the Swiss Federal Criminal Court in Bellinzona delivered a historic verdict against Trafigura, marking the first time a company was held criminally liable for the bribery of foreign public officials in a public trial in Switzerland. The court found that between April 2009 and October 2011, Trafigura orchestrated a complex scheme to pay nearly $5 million in bribes to Paulo Gouveia Júnior, a senior official at the Angolan state oil company, Sonangol.

The illicit payments were facilitated through a network of intermediaries and offshore shell companies. In exchange for the bribes, Trafigura secured highly lucrative shipping and bunker contracts from Sonangol, from which the company allegedly reaped approximately $145 million in illicit profits.

Convictions and Penalties

The court handed down significant penalties for both the corporation and the individuals involved. Trafigura as an entity was ordered to pay a fine of 3 million Swiss francs (approximately $3.3 million) and was mandated to set aside $145 million for potential compensation claims stemming from the illicitly obtained profits.

On the individual level, the court convicted three key figures: Mike Wainwright, the former Chief Operating Officer of Trafigura, was sentenced to 32 months in prison (12 months to be served) for organizing the payments. Paulo Gouveia Júnior, the Angolan official, received a 36-month sentence (14 months to be served) for passive bribery. Thierry Plojoux, a former Trafigura employee acting as an intermediary, received a fully suspended 24-month sentence.

Analytical Context

The conviction of Trafigura represents a watershed moment in the prosecution of transnational corporate corruption. Historically, major commodities traders operating out of jurisdictions like Switzerland have faced criticism for navigating weak regulatory environments and utilizing opaque intermediaries to extract resources from developing nations with minimal accountability.

This ruling pierces the corporate veil, establishing a legal precedent for holding massive trading houses criminally liable for the actions of their executives in the global south. It highlights the systemic mechanisms—offshore accounts, shell companies, and high-level political capture—used by the financial and corporate apex to secure monopolies over critical national resources, fitting squarely into broader discussions on corporate sovereignty and the extraction of wealth from sovereign states.
